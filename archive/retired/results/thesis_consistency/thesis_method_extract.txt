This study employed a quantitative retrospective cohort design to examine the effectiveness of machine learning (ML) algorithms in chronic disease management and their potential to improve patient outcomes. 4.2.2 Data Transformation

Following data cleaning, transformation procedures were applied to convert the dataset into a format suitable for machine learning analysis. These procedures ensured that variables were represented numerically and that differences in measurement scales did not distort model estimation or predictive performance.

Continuous variables, including age, BMI, systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, HbA1c, cholesterol, creatinine, medication adherence, length of stay, prior admissions, and comorbidity count, were standardized using z-score normalization.

z = (x − μ) / σ

In this equation, x represents the original observation, μ denotes the  mean  of the variable, and σ represents the standard deviation. Standardization centered the variables around zero and rescaled them to unit variance, thereby ensuring comparability among predictors measured on different scales. This procedure was applied specifically to Support Vector Machine and Artificial Neural Network models because these algorithms are sensitive to differences in feature magnitude. In contrast, Random Forest and XGBoost models were trained using the original unscaled variables since tree-based methods are generally unaffected by scale variation.   Categorical variables were transformed into numerical forms prior to model fitting. Binary variables such as sex and smoking status were encoded using 0/1 representation, while disease type, consisting of diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease categories, was transformed through one-hot encoding. This approach enabled the contribution of each disease category to be estimated independently without introducing artificial ordinal relationships among the categories.

                   4.4 Performance Evaluation of Machine Learning Models

4.4.1 Training and Validation Process

The final dataset consisted of 1,600 cleaned and preprocessed patient records obtained from sixteen regional hospitals in Ghana. To support robust model development and unbiased performance assessment, the dataset was divided into a training set and a holdout test set using a stratified 70:30 split. This resulted in 1,120 records (70.0%) allocated to the training set and 480 records (30.0%) reserved exclusively for final testing. The training set was utilized for model fitting, class balancing using SMOTE, and hyperparameter optimization, while the holdout test set was kept completely untouched until the final evaluation phase. This separation is critical in predictive modeling as it simulates real-world deployment conditions, allowing for an honest assessment of how the models would perform on new, unseen patient data.

Stratified sampling was deliberately applied during the data split to preserve the original proportions of positive and negative outcomes for 30-day readmission, disease progression, and complications in both subsets. Given the inherent class imbalance in the outcome variables, this stratification ensured that both the training and test sets remained representative of the full dataset. By maintaining consistent outcome distributions, the approach minimized sampling bias and prevented situations where one subset might artificially contain easier or harder cases, thereby enhancing the reliability of model performance estimates.

Within the training dataset, stratified 5-fold cross-validation was conducted to provide a robust estimate of model stability and to guide hyperparameter tuning. The 1,120 training records were divided into five approximately equal folds of 224 records each. In every iteration, four folds (896 records) were used to train the model while the held-out fold (224 records) served as the internal validation set. This process was repeated five times so that every record in the training set was used exactly once for validation. Averaging the performance metrics across the five folds offered a more reliable indication of model behavior than a single train-validation split and played a vital role in reducing the risk of overfitting, especially important when working with clinical data that may contain subtle patterns and noise.

Hyperparameter optimization was performed using  Optuna , a powerful Bayesian optimization framework.  Optuna  efficiently explored a wide range of parameter combinations for each algorithm (Random Forest, XGBoost, Support Vector Machine, and Neural Network) with the goal of maximizing predictive performance on the cross-validation folds. This systematic search was essential because different algorithms have unique sensitivities to parameters such as tree depth, learning rate, regularization strength, and kernel functions. The combination of stratified 5-fold cross-validation and  Optuna  optimization ensured that the final models were not only well-tuned but also stable across different subsets of the training data.

Overall, the adopted training and validation framework, comprising a stratified holdout split, stratified k-fold cross-validation, and Bayesian hyperparameter optimization, provided a rigorous and methodologically sound foundation for model development. These practices are particularly important in healthcare predictive modeling, where model reliability, generalizability, and avoidance of overfitting can have direct implications for clinical decision support. By implementing these steps, the study established a strong basis for fair comparison among the four algorithms and increased confidence in the final performance results obtained on the independent holdout test set.

4.4.2 Performance Metrics

The performance of the developed machine learning models was evaluated using five widely accepted classification metrics: accuracy, precision, recall, F1-score, and AUC-ROC. These metrics were selected because they provide a comprehensive and multidimensional assessment of model performance, which is particularly crucial in healthcare settings where both false positives and false negatives can have significant clinical consequences.

Accuracy measures the overall proportion of correct predictions. Precision indicates the proportion of positive predictions that were correct. Recall (sensitivity) measures the proportion of actual positive cases correctly identified. The F1-score, as the harmonic means of precision and recall, offers a balanced view especially useful for imbalanced datasets. The AUC-ROC evaluates the model’s ability to discriminate between classes across different thresholds.

The model also achieved a recall of 89.3%, demonstrating its ability to correctly identify  the majority of  patients who eventually experienced adverse health outcomes. From a clinical perspective, high recall is essential because failure to identify high-risk patients may result in missed opportunities for preventive interventions and timely treatment. The F1-score of 0.918 further confirms the effectiveness of  XGBoost  by demonstrating an excellent balance between precision and recall. Most notably, the model achieved an AUC-ROC value of 0.985, indicating near-perfect discrimination between high-risk and low-risk patients. This suggests that  XGBoost  consistently ranked patients according to their actual risk levels regardless of the classification threshold applied. The superior performance of  XGBoost  can be attributed to its gradient boosting mechanism, which sequentially builds decision trees while correcting errors made by previous trees. Additionally, features such as regularization, shrinkage, column subsampling, and efficient handling of missing values help reduce overfitting and improve model generalizability. These characteristics make  XGBoost  particularly effective in healthcare datasets where complex nonlinear interactions exist among variables such as age, blood pressure, blood glucose levels, cholesterol, body mass index, medication adherence, smoking status, and comorbidities.

Random Forest ranked second among the evaluated algorithms. The model achieved an accuracy of 90.2%, precision of 88.4%, recall of  85.5%, F1-score of 0.869, and AUC-ROC of 0.963. These results indicate strong predictive capability and excellent discrimination. Random Forest operates by constructing multiple decision trees using bootstrap samples and random subsets of predictor variables, before combining the results through majority voting. This ensemble approach reduces variance, increases stability, and improves robustness against overfitting. The algorithm was particularly effective at capturing nonlinear relationships and interactions among clinical variables without requiring assumptions about underlying data distributions. Despite its strong performance, Random Forest consistently produced lower scores than  XGBoost  across all evaluation metrics. The difference was especially evident in recall, where Random Forest identified fewer high-risk patients. This limitation may be attributed to the fact that Random Forest builds trees independently and lacks the iterative error-correction mechanism that characterizes boosting algorithms. Nevertheless, the model’s high AUC-ROC value demonstrates that it remains a reliable tool for healthcare prediction and could be considered for implementation in settings where model stability and interpretability are prioritized. The Support Vector Machine (SVM) also demonstrated competitive performance, achieving an accuracy of 89.9%, precision of 87.4%, recall of 85.7%, F1-score of 0.865, and AUC-ROC of 0.961. These findings suggest that SVM was effective in distinguishing between high-risk and low-risk patients. By identifying an optimal hyperplane that maximizes class separation, SVM performs well when complex relationships can be represented within a transformed feature space. The use of standardized clinical variables enhanced the model’s ability to process continuous indicators such as blood pressure, glucose levels, and cholesterol measurements. However, SVM achieved slightly lower precision and recall compared with the ensemble tree-based models. This suggests that the algorithm faced challenges in fully capturing the heterogeneous nature of chronic disease profiles, where interactions among clinical, behavioural, and socioeconomic variables may be highly complex. Although kernel functions allow SVM to model nonlinear relationships, its performance remains sensitive to parameter selection and kernel optimization.  Consequently, its ability to balance false positives and false negatives was somewhat lower than that of  XGBoost  and Random Forest.

The Neural Network achieved an accuracy of 89.6%, precision of 86.7%, recall of 85.8%, F1-score of 0.863, and AUC-ROC of 0.959. These results indicate that the model successfully identified meaningful patterns within the healthcare dataset and performed comparably to SVM. Neural networks are particularly valuable because they can capture complex nonlinear relationships and higher-order interactions among predictor variables. In this study, the model effectively learned associations among demographic, metabolic, cardiovascular, and behavioural factors that influence chronic disease outcomes. Despite these strengths, the Neural Network recorded the lowest overall performance among the evaluated models. One possible explanation is that neural networks typically require larger datasets to fully realize their predictive capabilities. Additionally, model performance is highly dependent on architecture design, parameter initialization, learning rates, and regularization techniques. Another limitation is reduced interpretability, which can hinder clinician trust and acceptance when healthcare decisions require transparency and justification. Overall, all four machine learning algorithms demonstrated excellent predictive performance, with AUC-ROC values ranging from 0.959 to 0.985. The relatively narrow performance differences among Random Forest, SVM, and Neural Network indicate that the dataset contained strong predictive signals that could be effectively utilized by different modelling approaches. Nevertheless,  XGBoost  consistently outperformed the other algorithms across every evaluation metric, confirming its superiority for predicting chronic disease outcomes. These findings have important implications for healthcare practice. Effective clinical decision-support systems must minimize both false-positive and false-negative predictions. While false positives can result in unnecessary healthcare interventions and increased costs, false negatives may prevent high-risk patients from receiving timely preventive care. By achieving the highest precision and recall,  XGBoost  demonstrated the most favourable balance between these competing considerations, making it the most clinically useful model. The results also validate the methodological rigor of the study. The strong performance achieved  across all models reflects the effectiveness of preprocessing procedures, including data cleaning, feature scaling, categorical encoding, class balancing using SMOTE, stratified train-test splitting, and hyperparameter optimization. Collectively, the findings support existing literature that identifies  XGBoost  as one of the most effective machine learning algorithms for healthcare prediction. Furthermore, the study provides valuable evidence from Ghanaian healthcare data, demonstrating the potential of predictive analytics and machine learning to improve chronic disease management, enhance clinical decision-making, and support early intervention strategies within local healthcare settings.

4.5 SHAP Analysis Results

The SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) analysis was conducted to provide a detailed interpretation of feature contributions to the predictions generated by the machine learning models, particularly the best-performing XGBoost model. SHAP values quantify the extent to which each feature contributes to increasing or decreasing the predicted probability of an outcome for each individual patient, thereby offering both global and local interpretability. The SHAP summary indicated that systolic blood pressure had the highest overall contribution to model predictions. Higher SHAP values for systolic blood pressure were consistently associated with increased predicted risk of adverse outcomes, while lower values corresponded to reduced risk. This demonstrates a strong positive relationship between elevated blood pressure and the likelihood of complications, hospital readmission, or disease progression. HbA1c levels were also identified as a dominant predictor. The SHAP distribution showed that higher HbA1c values significantly pushed model predictions toward higher risk categories, particularly for diabetic patients. Conversely, controlled HbA1c levels contributed negatively to risk prediction, indicating protective effects. This reinforces its clinical relevance as a marker of long-term  glycaemic  control. Age demonstrated a steady upward trend in SHAP impact, with older patients consistently receiving higher risk contributions from this variable. This pattern indicates that age acts as a cumulative risk amplifier across all chronic disease categories. Similarly, BMI showed a nonlinear relationship, where both moderate and high values contributed positively to risk, though the effect was more pronounced at higher levels, reflecting obesity-related complications. Creatinine levels showed strong SHAP influence in distinguishing patients at risk of renal and cardiovascular complications. Elevated creatinine values consistently increased predicted risk scores, indicating reduced kidney function as a critical determinant in disease progression models. Smoking status and cholesterol levels exhibited moderate but consistent SHAP effects. Smoking increased risk contributions across cardiovascular outcomes, while elevated cholesterol values contributed positively to risk predictions, though with less magnitude compared to blood pressure and HbA1c.

                  4.5.1 Most Significant Predictors

To identify the most important predictors of chronic disease outcomes, this study employed SHAP ( SHapley  Additive Explanations) analysis and feature importance rankings derived from machine learning models. Predictors were categorized into three groups: demographic factors, clinical indicators, and comorbidities. The analysis revealed that each group contributed differently to predictive performance and patient risk stratification. Demographic variables provided a foundational layer of prediction across all machine learning algorithms. Among these variables, age emerged as the most influential demographic predictor. SHAP analysis showed a clear trend in which increasing age was associated with a higher probability of hospital readmission, disease progression, and chronic disease complications. This relationship can be attributed to age-related physiological decline and prolonged exposure to chronic disease risk factors. Gender also contributed to prediction, although its influence was less pronounced. Male patients generally exhibited higher risk levels for cardiovascular-related outcomes, while gender differences in diabetes-related outcomes were more balanced. Overall, demographic variables functioned primarily as background risk modifiers rather than direct determinants of disease outcomes. Clinical indicators were identified as the most significant predictors, producing the highest SHAP values across all models. Systolic blood pressure emerged as the strongest clinical predictor, with elevated levels substantially increasing the likelihood of adverse outcomes. Diastolic blood pressure also demonstrated a meaningful, though slightly weaker, influence. HbA1c levels were particularly important in diabetes prediction, as higher values indicated poor glycaemic control and increased risk of complications. Body mass index (BMI) showed a strong nonlinear relationship with risk, especially at higher obesity levels. Additionally, cholesterol and creatinine levels played critical roles in cardiovascular and renal risk assessment, respectively. These findings highlight the direct physiological relationship between clinical indicators and chronic disease progression. Comorbidities further enhanced predictive accuracy by capturing the effects of multiple coexisting conditions. Patients with combinations of diabetes, hypertension, and cardiovascular disease consistently exhibited  higher risk scores. Smoking status also contributed significantly to risk prediction, particularly for cardiovascular outcomes. The interaction between smoking and other clinical factors further increased predicted risk levels. Overall, the results indicate that clinical indicators were the most influential predictors, followed by comorbidities, while demographic factors provided essential baseline information for risk assessment.

4.5.2 Implications of Key Predictive Features

In chronic disease management, understanding the predictive variables that identify risk factors and impact the course of the disease is critical to improving early identification of risk factors, monitoring of patients, and selecting treatment interventions for chronic diseases. The key predictive variables for chronic disease management were identified as clinical indicators (e.g., systolic blood pressure, HbA1c, BMI, cholesterol, and creatinine values) combined with socio-demographic and co-morbidity-related (e.g., age, sex, ethnicity, and number of concomitant chronic conditions) predictors that have value in generating predictable risk profiles for patients in healthcare systems using machine learning. This study will assist providers in managing chronic diseases because it shows how continuously monitored clinical indicators can predict the progression of chronic disease conditions and allow for appropriate interventions. For instance, patients with hypertension and increased levels of HbA1c were identified as having an increased risk of complications or readmission to the hospital. Therefore, monitoring patients with uncontrolled hypertension and/or diabetes should be more frequent. Similarly, BMI and cholesterol are 2 examples of how lifestyle factors and metabolic control affect chronic disease management over time. Creatinine is also a useful indicator, as it can show an early sign of kidney impairment, which is a common complication of hypertension and diabetes. By using these clinical indicators in predictive models, healthcare organizations can transition to a proactive approach to chronic disease management by identifying patients with high co-morbidity risk before they deteriorate clinically. In terms of providing support for clinicians' clinical decision-making process, the predictive features identified in this study will enhance the interpretability and usefulness of machine learning models for clinical use. Clinicians can utilize these variables as structured risk  indicators when determining treatment priority and resource allocation. For example, clinicians could use the SHAP contribution score from hypertension and HbA1c to flag a patient as requiring monitoring more frequently than those without elevated SHAP contributions. The interpretability provided by SHAP analysis further strengthens clinical trust in the model outputs, as it allows healthcare professionals to understand why a specific prediction was made. This transparency supports integration into clinical workflows, particularly in decision-support systems where explainability is essential.
